DELIVERABLE 2 — KEYNOTE NARRATIVE ARCHITECTURE

For the highest-priority talk, build the full narrative. Every section must include exact language, not outlines:

**Section 1 — OPENING HOOK (0–3 min)**
Three options:
- Option A (Provocative Statistic): A data point that contradicts the audience's assumptions. Include exact opening line and source.
- Option B (Pattern-Interrupt Story): 90-second personal story with a counterintuitive lesson. Include the moment of surprise.
- Option C (Rhetorical Question): A question the audience will answer incorrectly — then reveal why. Include the question and the reframe.

**Section 2 — THE PROBLEM REFRAME (3–10 min)**
Identify the "wrong way" everyone is thinking about this problem. Structure:
- The Prevailing Belief: "Most [persona] believe that [common assumption]"
- The Evidence of Failure: 2–3 data points showing this approach isn't working
- The Real Problem: "The actual issue isn't [symptom], it's [root cause]"
- Stakes: Who is hurt, what it costs them in real terms (revenue, time, career risk)
Use the "Before State" framework — make the problem world vivid and uncomfortable.

**Section 3 — THE PROPRIETARY FRAMEWORK (10–22 min)**
Introduce the executive's named model or system. Requirements:
- Give it a memorable name (3–4 words, acronym optional)
- Create a visual metaphor that can be drawn on a whiteboard in 30 seconds
- Break into 3–5 named components
- For each component: what it is (plain language), why it matters (cost of ignoring it), what "good" looks like (a specific benchmark or behavior)
- Include one "aha" moment per component — the insight the audience didn't have before

**Section 4 — PROOF (22–27 min)**
Two customer stories structured using SITUATION-STRUGGLE-SOLUTION-RESULT:
- Story 1: Mid-market company, relatable struggle, dramatic quantified result
- Story 2: Enterprise company or high-credibility brand name, complex implementation, strategic outcome
For each: Explain WHY the result happened (not just what happened) — this builds conviction, not just hope. Include the specific metric, timeline, and what changed in how they worked.

**Section 5 — VISION + CALL TO ACTION (27–30 min)**
- 18-month prediction: 3 specific, falsifiable predictions about where this category goes
- "The world where this is universal": Paint the picture of the positive future state
- CTA: One low-friction action with immediate value (assessment tool, research report, community access — never "request a demo" as the primary CTA from stage)
- Closing line: A single sentence the audience will repeat to colleagues

## Troubleshooting

**Problem: Talk abstracts keep getting rejected by conference program committees**
Solution: Most rejections happen because abstracts read as product pitches dressed up as education. Run this test: count the number of times you use "we" (company as subject) vs. "you" (audience as subject) — the ratio should be at least 80% audience-centric. Remove all product feature references. Replace with: a specific, data-backed claim in the first sentence, a named framework the audience can use regardless of your product, and a falsifiable prediction or counterintuitive thesis. Committees reject vendor pitches; they accept intellectual contributions.

**Problem: Large conference audience but zero pipeline generated**
Solution: The pipeline failure is almost always a missing "bridge" between educational content and commercial intent. Diagnose: (1) Was there a specific CTA with immediate tangible value (not "contact sales")? (2) Was there an email capture mechanism beyond business cards? (3) Was there a same-day follow-up within 6 hours? Most conference pipeline is lost in the 48 hours after the talk when enthusiasm is highest. Implement the Day-0 sequence before the conference, not after. The resource landing page must be live and the follow-up email must be drafted before the executive takes the stage.

**Problem: The executive's talk is forgettable — no differentiation from other speakers**
Solution: Apply the "Competitor Stage Test": state the executive's core thesis, then ask "Would a direct competitor on the same stage say the exact opposite?" If the answer is no — the thesis isn't differentiated enough to be memorable. Great B2B conference talks are remembered because they take a clear, defensible position that creates intellectual friction. The goal is not to please everyone in the room — it is to be the talk people argue about at dinner and reference in Slack channels the following week.
