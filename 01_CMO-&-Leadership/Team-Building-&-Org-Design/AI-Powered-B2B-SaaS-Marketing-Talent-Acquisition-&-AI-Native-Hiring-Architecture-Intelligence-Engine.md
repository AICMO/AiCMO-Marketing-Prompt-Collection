### SECTION 3 — INTERVIEW PROCESS DESIGN

**3A. Interview Process Architecture**

Design a 4-stage process that makes a final decision in ≤ 3 weeks from first application:

| Stage | Format | Duration | Interviewer | What It Reveals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Application Screen | Async written question (1 response required) | 15 min for candidate | Recruiter / CMO async review | Communication quality, attention to detail, AI tool literacy |
| Stage 2: Phone Screen | Video call | 30 min | CMO or Hiring Manager | Culture fit, career motivation, compensation alignment, actual experience validation |
| Stage 3: AI Skills Assessment | Take-home work sample | 60 min (hard cap) | Evaluated by CMO + 1 team member | Actual job skill, AI tool fluency, output quality under realistic conditions |
| Stage 4: Interview Loop | Back-to-back live interviews | 90 min total | 3 interviewers (CMO + cross-functional) | Depth in role-specific areas, cross-functional collaboration style, revenue orientation |

**3B. Application Screen Question (Filter 80% of Unqualified Candidates)**

Do not accept applications without a required written response. Use this format:

**Application Question:** "In 3–5 sentences, describe the demand generation experiment you're most proud of running in the last 18 months. Include: what you tested, what the result was (in numbers), and what you learned that you've applied to your work since. Note: 'we ran a webinar series' is not an experiment — we're looking for a hypothesis, a variable, and an outcome."

**What a strong response looks like:**
- Includes a specific hypothesis ("We believed that LinkedIn outperforming Google Ads was due to audience quality, not intent volume")
- Has a numeric result ("We 3x'd pipeline/$ from LinkedIn while reducing Google Ads spend by 30%")
- Includes an actionable learning ("We now build custom LinkedIn matched audiences from our Salesforce ICP accounts before launching any new LinkedIn campaign")

**What a weak response looks like (auto-reject triggers):**
- No numbers
- Activity described, not outcome ("We launched LinkedIn Ads and saw good engagement")
- Uses "we" exclusively — unclear what the candidate personally contributed
- Longer than 200 words (poor communication discipline)

**3C. Stage 2 Phone Screen — Question Framework**

| Category | Question | What a Strong Answer Includes |
|---|---|---|
| AI tool literacy | "Walk me through a specific marketing task you now do with AI that you used to do manually. How long did it take manually vs. with AI? What's the quality difference?" | Specific tool named, specific time savings cited, honest about quality tradeoff, shows this is a habit not a one-time experiment |
| Revenue orientation | "Tell me about the last time a marketing program you ran was underperforming. How did you diagnose the problem and what did you do about it?" | Talks about pipeline impact, not engagement metrics; shows diagnostic thinking not just execution; takes ownership |
| Intellectual honesty | "What's the marketing experiment you ran that failed most clearly? What did you learn from it?" | Can articulate a real failure without deflecting; learning is actionable not platitudinous; shows experimental mindset |
| Culture fit | "Describe your ideal working relationship with a sales team. What do you need from them, and what should they expect from you?" | Talks about shared pipeline accountability; shows understanding of SDR/AE workflow; flags if they view sales as a separate entity |
| Motivation | "Why are you looking now, and why does this role specifically interest you?" | References specific things from the job description; not just "opportunity for growth"; shows they researched the company |

**3D. Stage 4 Interview Loop — Structured Scorecard**

Each interviewer scores the candidate on ONE primary dimension:

**Interview 1: CMO — Strategic Thinking & Revenue Orientation (45 min)**

| Dimension | Weight | Score (1–5) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue clarity | 30% | | |
| Experimental mindset | 25% | | |
| AI fluency depth | 25% | | |
| Self-awareness | 20% | | |

Key Questions:
1. "If you joined tomorrow and had 90 days to prove you were the right hire, what would you do in days 1–30, 31–60, and 61–90?"
   - **5 answer:** Specific: "I'd spend the first two weeks mapping the existing pipeline attribution model against actual CRM data, identify the top 2 sources of data contamination, and propose a clean data model before touching any programs. Then I'd run a 14-day spend pause experiment on our lowest-efficiency paid channel while we reconfigure targeting."
   - **2 answer:** Generic: "I'd listen and learn, meet the team, and understand the business before making changes."

2. "Our CMO dashboard currently tracks 23 marketing metrics. As Head of Demand Gen, what's the one metric you'd hold yourself accountable to above all others, and why?"
   - **5 answer:** "Marketing-sourced pipeline created per dollar of combined marketing spend — because that single metric forces me to balance volume and quality, and it's the one the CFO and CRO can't argue with in any budget conversation."
   - **2 answer:** "MQL volume, because it shows we're generating leads at top of funnel."

3. "Describe how you'd use AI to make our existing demand gen programs 30% more efficient in 60 days — without adding budget."
   - **5 answer:** Names specific tools and specific tasks (e.g., "I'd run all our ICP account lists through Clay with a waterfall enrichment flow to remove non-ICP accounts before launching LinkedIn campaigns, which typically reduces wasted spend by 15–20%")
   - **2 answer:** "I'd use ChatGPT to write better ad copy and maybe automate some email sequences."

**Interview 2: CRO or Head of Sales — Marketing-Sales Alignment (30 min)**

Key Questions:
1. "When a sales rep tells you a lead is bad quality, how do you typically respond?"
2. "How do you define the difference between a marketing sourced lead and a marketing influenced lead? Why does it matter?"
3. "Describe a time you changed a marketing program because sales gave you feedback. What was the specific feedback and what exactly changed?"

Scoring Focus: Does this candidate instinctively collaborate with sales or defend against them? Do they understand pipeline from a revenue operations perspective? Are they comfortable being held accountable to pipeline numbers, not just MQL numbers?

**Interview 3: Cross-Functional Peer (RevOps/Content/Product Marketing) — Collaboration & Communication (15 min)**

Key Questions:
1. "Tell me about a cross-functional project you led where something went wrong. How did you handle it?"
2. "What's the most important information you need from RevOps/Product Marketing/the Content team to do your best work — and how do you get it when they're busy?"

Scoring Focus: Is this person easy to work with, direct in their communication, and clear about what they need vs. what they'll figure out themselves?

### SECTION 4 — AI SKILLS WORK SAMPLE

**4A. Take-Home Assessment Design Principles**

Rules for an effective AI skills work sample:
- Hard cap at 60 minutes — penalize submissions that clearly took longer (shows poor prioritization)
- The output must be something the team would actually use (not a hypothetical)
- Evaluate on quality of thinking + AI usage, not just output polish
- Send at the same time to every candidate — do not customize
- Provide access to all AI tools they want to use (if you restrict, you're testing effort, not skill)

**4B. Sample Work Sample: Head of Demand Generation Role**

**Assessment Brief (send to candidate):**

"This work sample is designed to take 60 minutes. Please use whatever AI tools you'd normally use in your day-to-day work — we want to see how you work with AI, not without it.

**Context:** Arco Labs sells AI-powered revenue forecasting software to Series A–C SaaS CFOs. Our ACV is $24K–$72K ARR. We have $22K/month to spend on LinkedIn Ads and Google Ads combined.

**Your Task:** Using publicly available information + any AI tools you choose:

1. (20 min) Build a LinkedIn Ads audience targeting strategy for one specific CFO persona segment at Series B SaaS companies ($10M–$30M ARR). Include: job title targeting, company criteria, content themes, and why you'd expect this audience to outperform a broader CFO audience. Format as a brief (200–300 words + a 5-row targeting spec table).

2. (20 min) Write 3 LinkedIn ad variants (Headline + Body + CTA) targeting the same audience. Each variant should test a different message angle. Format each as: Angle Name → Headline (under 25 chars) → Body (under 150 chars) → CTA.

3. (20 min) Propose a 30-day test structure: how would you split $22K across LinkedIn and Google this month, what would you measure at the end of 30 days to decide whether to scale or restructure, and what's your decision rule? (150–200 words)

Note: We're evaluating your thinking and your AI usage — not production-perfect copy. Show your work or tell us what AI you used and how."

**Evaluation Rubric:**

| Dimension | Weight | 5 (Excellent) | 3 (Adequate) | 1 (Poor) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Audience specificity | 25% | Audience is precisely defined with specific job functions, seniority levels, company size range, and at least one non-obvious insight about the persona | Audience is correctly defined but generic (all CFOs at SaaS companies) | Audience is wrong persona, too broad, or shows no B2B SaaS understanding |
| Ad variant quality | 25% | Three distinct message angles, each with a testable hypothesis; headlines under character limit; copy feels like it was written for the human, not the machine | Two distinct angles; copy is adequate but not compelling; character limits respected | One angle repeated with surface variation; copy reads like AI output that wasn't edited |
| Test structure | 25% | Clear success metric per variant, pre-defined decision rules ("if X, we do Y"), realistic budget split with rationale, acknowledges data volume needed for significance | Reasonable test structure but unclear decision rules or success criteria | Activity-based test with no clear hypothesis or decision criteria |
| AI usage quality | 25% | Evidence of smart prompting (persona-informed prompts, iterative refinement, AI used to speed up not replace thinking) | AI used but no evidence of customization or critical evaluation of AI output | No AI used, or AI output pasted unedited with no evident judgment applied |

### SECTION 5 — REFERENCE CHECK PROTOCOL

**5A. Reference Check Architecture**

**Who to call:** Always call references the candidate didn't provide. Ask the candidate: "Who was your direct manager at [Company] for [dates]?" Then find that person on LinkedIn and contact them independently — not through the candidate. The difference in what you hear when you call unreferenced managers is significant.

**Reference Check Questions (30-minute call structure):**

| Timing | Question | What You're Actually Measuring |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 min | "How did you work with [Candidate] and in what capacity?" | Verify the relationship and title accuracy |
| 5–10 min | "What would you say [Candidate] was genuinely best at — the thing they were known for on your team?" | First positive anchor — listen for specificity vs. generics |
| 10–15 min | "If you hired [Candidate] again tomorrow, what role would you put them in — and what role would you NOT put them in?" | This question reveals their honest assessment of the candidate's ceiling |
| 15–20 min | "On a scale of 1–10, how would you rate their ability to work well with a sales team? Can you give me a specific example that informed that rating?" | Marketing-sales collaboration is a non-negotiable predictor for pipeline-owning roles |
| 20–25 min | "What's the most significant professional growth you saw [Candidate] make while working with you?" | Reveals coachability and growth trajectory |
| 25–30 min | "Is there anything I should know that would help set this person up for success in a role like this one?" | Open-ended final question — many references save their most candid observation for here |

**Red flags in reference calls:**
- Reference pauses before answering positive questions
- Uses only superlatives with zero specifics ("incredibly talented," "best I've ever worked with")
- Fails to name a specific achievement when asked
- Says "I'd rehire them" but qualifies with "in the right role"
- Cannot describe what the candidate contributed specifically vs. what the team accomplished

**6B. Variable Compensation Structure for Pipeline-Owning Roles**

Tie variable compensation directly to the metric the role owns:

| Metric | Weight | Performance Threshold | Payout |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marketing-sourced pipeline created | 50% | 100% of quarterly target | 100% of variable |
| Marketing-sourced pipeline created | 50% | 80–99% of target | 80% of variable |
| Marketing-sourced pipeline created | 50% | <80% of target | 0% variable |
| MQL-to-SQL rate (quality metric) | 30% | ≥ 45% quarterly average | Full payout |
| CAC efficiency improvement | 20% | Blended CAC decreases QoQ | Payout at 5% intervals |

Do not tie variable compensation to MQL volume alone — it incentivizes quantity over quality and creates friction with sales.

**6C. Closing a Top Candidate — Offer Frame**

When extending an offer to a top candidate, deliver it using this framework:

**1. Lead with impact, not compensation:**
"Before I give you the numbers, I want to make sure you understand what this role is actually about. You'd be the second marketing hire at a company doing $12M ARR with a clear path to $30M+ in the next 18 months. The pipeline target you'd own directly represents 40% of our annual growth plan — your fingerprints are on our series C story. That's either exciting to you or it isn't."

**2. Present compensation as a complete package:**
"Here's the full picture: $140K base, 15% variable tied entirely to pipeline you create (so at 100% attainment, you're at $161K total cash), and 0.20% options in a company that last raised at a $38M valuation. At our current growth trajectory, that equity has real upside."

**3. Address competing offers directly:**
"If you're comparing this to an offer at a larger company, here's what I'd encourage you to think about: at [BigComp], you'll manage an existing playbook. Here, you'll build the one we copy for the next 10 years. Both are legitimate choices — just depends on what you want to be able to say about this chapter of your career."

**4. Create urgency without pressure:**
"We'd like to give you a week to make this decision. I'll be available for any follow-up questions. I do want to be transparent that we have one other finalist — if your decision timeline extends past [date], I need to let them know where things stand."

**Success criteria for the hire at Day 90:**

- At least 2 completed experiments with documented learnings
- Zero surprises for CMO on pipeline — weekly reporting has been consistent and accurate
- Sales team relationship: SDR team leader should be able to name 1 specific thing this hire changed that made their leads better

## Troubleshooting

**Problem: You're getting lots of applicants but most are unqualified — time is wasted on screens that should have been rejected.**
Solution: Your job description is too generic or the application screen question is missing. Add the required written application question (Section 2A) — this single change typically reduces application volume by 50–70% while improving average quality. Also audit your LinkedIn job posting: if you're using phrases like "fast-growing startup" or "dynamic environment," you're attracting every candidate in the market. Replace with specific metrics and disqualifiers. If the "you are not a fit if" section is in your JD and you're still getting unqualified applicants, they're not reading past the title — shorten your JD to ≤ 600 words and front-load the disqualifiers.

**Problem: Candidates perform well in interviews but underperform in the first 90 days — a pattern repeating across multiple hires.**
Solution: Your interviews are measuring confidence and communication, not actual competency. Add the AI skills work sample (Section 4B) before the loop interview — if you haven't been using a work sample, this will immediately identify the gap. Also audit your Stage 4 interview questions: "tell me about a time you..." questions are gameable by well-coached candidates. Replace them with real-time problem solving: "We have this specific situation at Arco right now — [describe a real current challenge]. How would you approach it in your first 30 days?" Candidates who actually know the craft will give you a hypothesis and a test; candidates who've memorized interview playbooks will give you a framework with no specifics.

**Problem: You're losing top candidates to competing offers at larger companies — your offer is being rejected even when the candidate seemed excited.**
Solution: The offer is arriving too late in the process and the candidate has already mentally committed to the competing offer. Move the offer stage earlier: by Stage 3 (after the work sample), have a direct compensation conversation. Say: "If everything goes well in our final interview, we'd be looking at a range of [$X–$Y]. Is that in a range that would make sense for you?" This eliminates the awkward "we loved you but the number doesn't work" rejection. Also, don't present equity as an afterthought — build a one-page equity model showing the range of outcomes at 2x, 5x, and 10x valuation multiples. For A-players considering early-stage companies, equity story matters more than the base.
