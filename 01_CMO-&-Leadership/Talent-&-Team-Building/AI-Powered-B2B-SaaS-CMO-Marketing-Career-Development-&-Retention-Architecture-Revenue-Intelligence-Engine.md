## Overview
This prompt builds a complete, AI-powered career development and retention system for B2B SaaS marketing teams — from high-potential identification and individual development plans (IDPs) to retention risk scoring and succession readiness mapping. Use it when you're losing good marketers to competitors, struggling to promote from within, or when your team's growth trajectory is misaligned with your company's AI-era talent needs.

### SECTION 1: RETENTION RISK SCORECARD

**1.1 Flight Risk Signal Framework**

Score each team member across these dimensions monthly. Signals are weighted by predictive validity based on exit interview data patterns:

| Signal Category | Weight | High Risk Indicators (Score 3) | Medium Risk (Score 2) | Low Risk (Score 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Career trajectory clarity | 25% | "I don't see a path forward here" stated or implied; asked about promotion timeline 3+ times | Vague about future here; exploring options quietly | Clear about their next role internally; actively working toward it |
| Manager relationship quality | 20% | Skips 1:1s; complaints about manager surfaced in skip-levels; manager flagged as low-performer | Neutral about manager; limited engagement in 1:1s | Strong relationship; manager advocates for them; mentions manager as reason to stay |
| External market exposure | 20% | LinkedIn profile recently updated with keyword-optimized summary; has spoken to recruiters (you know this); external offer received in last 6 months | Growing LinkedIn network; attending industry events more; asking about market comp | Not active externally; expresses no interest in market exploration |
| Recognition & contribution clarity | 15% | Does not know how their work connects to company outcomes; hasn't been publicly recognized in 60+ days; was passed over for a project they wanted | Knows their metrics but doesn't feel recognized; occasional recognition | Regularly cited in all-hands, client calls, or leadership updates; clear line of sight to company impact |
| Compensation market alignment | 15% | Compensation is >15% below current market for their role/experience level | Within 5-15% of market; likely to encounter better offers | At or above market; has expressed satisfaction with comp |
| Team and environment engagement | 5% | Team dynamics have changed (manager left, peers left); cultural fit concerns; remote isolation | Engaged but quieter than usual | Actively engaged; participates in team building; recruits friends |

**Scoring Protocol:**
- Run this scorecard in your 1:1 prep 1x/month for Tier 1 employees
- Quarterly for Tier 2 employees
- Weighted average ≥ 2.3: Immediate retention conversation needed
- Weighted average 1.8-2.2: Schedule development conversation in next 30 days
- Weighted average < 1.8: Continue investing; monitor quarterly

**1.2 Retention Intervention Protocols by Risk Level**

**RED (Score ≥ 2.3) — Immediate Action Required:**

*Before you talk to them, answer these questions:*
- What does this person actually want that they're not getting? (career growth / compensation / recognition / different work / better manager)
- What can you realistically offer in the next 90 days? Be honest. Overpromising is worse than honesty.
- Is this a person you want to retain even if they're frustrated? (Not all retentions are the right call.)

*Retention conversation framework:*
1. Open with observation, not accusation: "I want to make sure you have what you need to do your best work here and that I understand what that means for you right now. Can we have an honest conversation about where things stand?"
2. Listen first — ask open questions about what's energizing them and what's frustrating them before offering anything
3. Share what you've observed and why you value them specifically — be concrete, not generic ("I noticed you've been less engaged in planning sessions" / "You built our ABM program from scratch and I need that kind of ownership on our next phase")
4. Make a specific offer (if you have something real): "Here's what I can do — [specific commitment with timeline]"
5. Be honest about what you can't offer: "What I can't promise right now is [thing they want] because [real reason]. Here's what that timeline looks like realistically."
6. Close with a specific next step: "Can we talk again in 2 weeks after I've [taken specific action]? I want to show you this isn't just words."

**YELLOW (Score 1.8-2.2) — Proactive Development Conversation:**

- Schedule a dedicated 45-minute "career conversation" separate from their regular 1:1
- Use the IDP framework in Section 3 to co-create a development plan
- Ensure they have a specific milestone in the next 90 days that moves them toward their stated career goal
- Follow up in their next two 1:1s on that milestone

**GREEN (Score < 1.8) — Maintain and Invest:**

- Continue regular 1:1 cadence
- Ensure they are actively growing — stagnation is the precursor to risk, even if they seem happy now
- Proactively connect them to stretch projects and visibility opportunities

### SECTION 2: CAREER ARCHITECTURE — DUAL-TRACK LADDER

**2.1 Why a Dual-Track Ladder Matters**

The most common reason high-performing ICs leave: they have to become managers to advance. If management is the only promotion path, you lose great ICs to management roles they don't actually want — producing bad managers AND losing great individual contributors. Build two parallel tracks with genuinely equivalent status and compensation.

**Marketing Career Levels — Individual Contributor Track:**

| Level | Title | Compensation Band | Scope | Promotion Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Marketing Associate / Coordinator | $60K-$80K | Executes defined tasks with guidance | [Time-based at L1: 12-18 months of solid execution] |
| L2 | Marketing Specialist | $80K-$100K | Owns specific programs with limited oversight | Manages at least 1 major program end-to-end; demonstrates AI-native workflow integration; independently improves a metric without being told to |
| L3 | Senior Marketing Specialist | $100K-$130K | Owns a function area; mentors L1-L2; cross-functional credibility | Owns a full channel or function; has measurably improved a key business metric; can represent marketing credibly in sales or executive meetings without CMO present |
| L4 | Lead / Staff Marketing Specialist | $130K-$160K | Equivalent to Manager in scope and compensation; sets strategy for their area; influences adjacent functions | Builds something the team uses at scale; influences company strategy; recognized externally (speaking, writing, community leadership) |
| L5 | Principal Marketing Specialist | $160K-$200K | Sets direction for multiple functions; operates like a functional GM for their area | Rarely hired, usually grown; has delivered a step-change in company outcomes tied directly to their work |

**Marketing Career Levels — Management Track:**

| Level | Title | Compensation Band | Scope | Promotion Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | Marketing Manager | $110K-$150K | Manages 2-4 ICs; owns a function | Has successfully mentored 2+ ICs; manages a team to metric targets for 6+ consecutive months; trusted to hire their own backfill |
| M2 | Senior Marketing Manager | $140K-$180K | Manages 4-7 people; may manage managers | Has built a function from < 3 to 5+ people; runs a team that consistently hits quarterly targets without CMO involvement; has developed 1 person for promotion |
| M3 | Director of Marketing | $170K-$220K | Manages a major function (demand gen / content / PMM); cross-functional ownership | Runs a team of 7-12; owns a $[X]M+ budget; has driven step-change improvement in a company-level metric; sits at VP-1 strategic decisions |
| M4 | VP of Marketing | $200K-$280K | Runs a major marketing division; CMO partner | Full P&L ownership for their area; has scaled a team through a company inflection point (Series A to B, B to C, or major product launch) |

**2.2 Promotion Decision Framework**

To prevent subjective, relationship-based promotion decisions (which create equity issues and retention risks among passed-over employees), use a documented promotion committee process:

*Promotion Eligibility Requirements (must meet ALL):*
1. **Time in role:** Minimum [X] months at current level (prevent premature promotions that set people up to fail)
2. **Performance threshold:** Rated "Meets Expectations" or above in last 2 performance cycles — no promotion for people currently on performance plans
3. **Level criteria met:** Must meet ≥ 80% of next-level criteria documented above — not "potential to meet," currently meeting
4. **360 readiness signal:** Peers and stakeholders who interact with them would not be surprised by the promotion

*Promotion Committee Process:*
1. Manager submits a 1-page promotion brief: current contributions, level criteria met, 1-2 things they still need to develop post-promotion, and comp recommendation
2. CMO and HR review: Is this person meeting criteria, or are we promoting because we're afraid to lose them? (Defensive promotion is a common failure mode — it sets the employee up to fail at the next level and creates comp compression for others)
3. Promotion conversation with the employee: Done in advance of official announcement. Tell them why they're being promoted — specifically. Not "you've been doing great" but "here's what you built, here's what it means for the company, and here's why we're investing in your next level."

### SECTION 3: INDIVIDUAL DEVELOPMENT PLAN FRAMEWORK

**3.1 IDP Design Principles**

*What makes IDPs fail:*
- Goals disconnected from business outcomes ("attend a conference," "improve communication skills")
- Annual cadence with no quarterly accountability checkpoints
- Employee writes it alone without manager investment — becomes a filing exercise
- CMO never reviews it — signals that development doesn't matter above the manager level

*What makes IDPs work:*
- Each development goal connects explicitly to a business outcome the employee cares about
- Goals are project-based, not time-based ("build and launch X by Q3" not "spend 3 months learning X")
- Manager commits to 3 specific enablers (resources, introductions, projects, feedback)
- Quarterly check-in is a real conversation, not a form review

**3.2 IDP Template — For Each Team Member**

**CURRENT STATE ASSESSMENT**

### SECTION 4: HIGH-POTENTIAL IDENTIFICATION & FAST-TRACK PROGRAM

**4.1 High-Potential Identification Criteria**

High-potential is NOT the same as high-performance. High-performers deliver great results at their current level. High-potentials can expand their scope significantly faster than their peers. Both are valuable — but they require different investment.

Identify your top 15-20% using these 4 criteria (must demonstrate all 4 to be designated HiPo):

| Criterion | What "HiPo" Looks Like | What "High-Performer (not HiPo)" Looks Like |
|---|---|---|
| Learning velocity | Absorbs new domains rapidly — 3 months into a new area they're already spotting patterns and proposing improvements | Strong in their established domain; slower to develop in new areas |
| Scope aspiration | Actively seeks responsibility beyond their defined role; volunteers for cross-functional problems | Excellent at owning defined scope; prefers depth over breadth |
| Influence without authority | Peers and cross-functional partners seek their input; they shape decisions in rooms they're not assigned to | Effective at executing on decisions made by others; less proactive about influencing direction |
| Resilience and adaptability | Their performance improves when conditions are ambiguous or difficult; they thrive in change | Performs best with defined goals and stable conditions |

**4.2 HiPo Fast-Track Program Design**

*For each HiPo employee, implement these 4 accelerants:*

**Accelerant 1: Executive Exposure**
- Quarterly 30-minute skip-level with CMO (or CEO for very senior HiPos) — specifically focused on their strategic thinking, not their function's metrics
- Assign them as the marketing representative to 1 cross-functional initiative (RevOps project, product launch planning, etc.) where they interact directly with C-suite or VP-level peers from other functions
- Include them in 1 board prep or investor meeting context per quarter — even as a note-taker initially; the pattern-matching they get from seeing how executives think about the business is irreplaceable

**Accelerant 2: Stretch Project Ownership**
- Assign them a project that is 1 level above their current scope with full ownership — they own the outcome, not just the execution
- Give them a budget (even small — $5K-$20K) they control independently — financial decision-making is a distinct skill that high-potentials need to develop before they're in roles where it's high-stakes
- After the project, conduct a deliberate debrief: what did they decide well? What would they do differently? This is where learning velocity is actually developed.

**Accelerant 3: External Platform**
- Sponsor them for 1 speaking opportunity or conference per year — external credibility builds internal credibility and protects against the "they're great but unknown outside our company" perception that limits promotions
- Encourage (and lightly require) 1 piece of external content per quarter — LinkedIn article, podcast appearance, community contribution — that demonstrates their expertise publicly
- Introduce them to 3 senior practitioners at peer companies (not competitors) — the peer network of their future level is built now, not after promotion

**Accelerant 4: Mentorship Architecture**
- Assign a senior internal mentor (you or another VP) who is outside their direct reporting line — gives them access to candid advice without the politics of the manager relationship
- Connect them to 1 external mentor from your network who has successfully navigated the transition they're trying to make — this is a meaningful gift that creates loyalty
- Document what the mentor and mentee agree to work on — this is not casual coffee; it's a structured 6-month engagement with defined goals

### SECTION 5: MANAGER DEVELOPMENT — THE RETENTION MULTIPLIER

**5.1 Why Manager Effectiveness Is Your Highest-Leverage Retention Variable**

Research is consistent: people leave managers, not companies. In B2B SaaS marketing, this is amplified — marketing managers are often promoted for their individual contributor performance, not their management capability. The result: technically strong managers who are emotionally unavailable, unable to develop their people, and who create the attrition problem they're supposed to prevent.

Your marketing managers need a development program too.

**5.2 Marketing Manager Effectiveness Assessment**

Assess each marketing manager you have against these 5 behaviors quarterly:

| Management Behavior | Green (Doing Well) | Yellow (Needs Development) | Red (Critical Gap) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 quality | 1:1s are consistent, manager-prepared, focused on employee's career AND current work; employee rates them valuable | 1:1s happen but are status updates; employee-led because manager isn't prepared | 1:1s are canceled frequently; used for task updates; employee dreads them |
| Development investment | Can name each direct report's IDP goals unprompted; takes active steps to create development opportunities | Is aware of IDPs but doesn't actively create conditions for goal achievement | IDPs exist on paper; no active investment; "they're fine" is the most specific thing they can say |
| Retention signal awareness | Can accurately predict which of their reports are at-risk and why; has already had proactive retention conversations | Aware of retention risk in general terms; hasn't had specific conversations | Surprised when people resign; "I didn't see it coming" |
| Recognition and feedback quality | Gives specific, timely, behavioral recognition ("What you did in that meeting — naming the attribution gap — changed how RevOps thinks about us"); gives direct developmental feedback | Recognition is generic ("great job"); developmental feedback is vague or withheld to avoid discomfort | Neither recognizes nor gives feedback; team isn't sure where they stand |
| AI-era leadership | Actively helps their team integrate AI into workflows; models AI-native behavior themselves; addresses AI anxiety directly in their 1:1s | Uses AI personally but hasn't brought it into their management practice; doesn't actively address team AI anxiety | Resistant to AI adoption; models manual work patterns; AI anxiety spreading through their team |

**5.3 Manager 1:1 Architecture That Retains Employees**

Give each of your marketing managers this 1:1 structure to follow with their reports:

*Recommended cadence: 45 minutes, weekly for Tier 1 and at-risk employees, biweekly for others*

**First 10 minutes: Employee-led check-in**
- "What's the most important thing on your mind right now — it doesn't have to be work-related?"
- "What's one thing from last week you're most proud of?"
- "What's your biggest frustration or blocker right now?"

*(Manager's job in this section: Listen. Don't solve yet. Ask follow-up questions. The goal is signal collection.)*

**Next 20 minutes: Work-focused discussion**
- "Walk me through where [specific project/metric] stands and what you need from me to keep it moving"
- "Is there anything you're working on where my perspective or network would be useful?"
- "What decision are you least sure about right now? Let's think through it together."

*(Manager's job: Coach, not direct. The goal is to build decision-making capability, not solve problems for them.)*

**Final 15 minutes: Career and development**
- Minimum once per month: "How are you feeling about your IDP goals? What's the current bottleneck on Goal [X]?"
- Once per quarter: "Zoom out for a second — how are you feeling about your career trajectory here? What would make the next 6 months feel like a significant step forward for you?"
- Occasionally: "Is there anything about how I'm managing you that's not working? I want to make sure I'm supporting you the right way."

*(This last section is the one most managers skip. It is also the one that surfaces retention risk before it becomes resignation.)*

### SECTION 6: SUCCESSION PLANNING

**6.1 Succession Risk Identification**

For each role on your marketing team, answer 3 questions:

1. **Business impact of vacancy:** If this person resigned tomorrow and we couldn't replace them for 90 days, what business outcome would break? (Rate 1-5, where 5 = pipeline collapse / major program failure)

2. **Time to replace externally:** How long would it realistically take to hire and onboard a qualified external replacement? (Ranges from 60 days for an IC to 6-9 months for a Director or VP)

3. **Internal succession readiness:** Do we have anyone internally who could step in — even as an interim — within 30 days? (Yes with 0-30 days of ramp / Yes with 60-90 days / No)

**Succession Priority Score = Business Impact × Time to Replace External ÷ Internal Readiness**

Focus succession investment on roles where: Business Impact ≥ 4 AND Internal Readiness = No.

**6.2 Succession Development Plan Template**

For each high-priority succession gap:

| Element | Details |
|---|---|
| Critical role | [e.g., VP of Demand Generation] |
| Current incumbent | [Name] | Estimated tenure risk: [High / Medium / Low] |
| Business impact if vacant | [Specific: "Pipeline generation drops 40% immediately as paid media budget goes unmanaged and ABM program stalls"] |
| Successor Option 1 | [Name] | Current role: [X] | Gap to readiness: [e.g., "Has functional expertise but has never managed a team or a $1M+ budget"] |
| Development actions for Successor 1 | [e.g., "Give them shadow access to VP's budget decisions for 60 days; assign them to lead one major program with VP as coach; enroll in people management training"] | Timeline to readiness: [X months] |
| Successor Option 2 (if exists) | [Name] | Gap: [describe] | Timeline: [X months] |
| External hire readiness | If both internal successors are 12+ months from readiness, begin building a warm external candidate pipeline now — don't wait for the role to open |

**Lattice / Leapsome / 15Five (People Management Platforms):**

- Configure automated quarterly IDP review reminders that go to both employee and manager — removes the "we forgot" failure mode that kills most development programs
- Use the platform's goal-tracking feature to log each IDP milestone with a target date — gives CMO a real-time dashboard of development progress across the team without requiring individual check-ins

**Notion (Career Architecture Hub):**
- Build a private Notion database with one page per employee containing: current IDP, retention risk score history, HiPo designation, and succession readiness notes — the CMO career intelligence hub
- Create a shared-with-employee version of their IDP in Notion that they can update with progress notes — signals that development is collaborative, not done to them
- Build a "Succession Board" Notion database visible only to CMO and HR that maps each critical role to internal successor options and their readiness timeline

**Culture Amp / Glint (Employee Engagement):**
- Run a quarterly pulse survey with 5 questions mapped to your retention risk signal categories — gives you quantified data that supplements qualitative manager signals
- Include 2 questions specifically on AI anxiety and AI capability development satisfaction — monitor quarter-over-quarter to see if your AI development investments are reducing anxiety
- Share anonymized team-level results with your marketing managers — makes retention a team-level accountability, not just a CMO concern

**Greenhouse / Lever (ATS — for Succession Pipeline):**
- Build a "Talent Pool" in your ATS for each critical role where you're managing a succession gap — add external candidates you meet at conferences, referrals from your network, and candidates who made it to final round in previous searches

## Troubleshooting

**Problem: Managers say they're doing 1:1s but employees in pulse surveys rate them as low-value status updates**
Solution: Your managers know how to have status update meetings — they need to learn how to have development and career conversations, which feel uncomfortable because they require vulnerability and admitting they don't have all the answers. Run a 2-hour "1:1 Skills Workshop" with your management team using the 1:1 structure from Section 5.3 — have them practice the "career and development" section specifically using a real employee (with consent) as the scenario. The discomfort they feel in the practice room is the same discomfort that prevents them from doing it in real 1:1s. Name it, practice through it, and give them permission to say "I don't know the answer to your career question, but I commit to figuring it out" — that response is more trust-building than a polished non-answer.

**Problem: High-potentials are being retained with title and comp increases but are plateauing because there's genuinely no room to grow at your stage**
Solution: This is a structural problem that compensation and titles can only defer, not solve. The honest answer is that your company stage may not be able to provide the scope a high-potential needs to grow at the rate they're capable of — and pretending otherwise with increasingly elaborate title inflation will eventually backfire when they recognize the scope isn't there. The alternative: lean into making them famous externally (speaking, writing, industry leadership) so that their growth accrues to their personal brand, not just to scope they don't have internally. Practitioners who are well-known externally often stay at companies where their role doesn't match their capability because the platform itself is valuable. Invest aggressively in your HiPos' external profiles — this is often more retention-valuable than a title bump.

**Problem: The retention risk scorecard is flagging people as at-risk but retention conversations are making them feel surveilled or like they're in trouble**
Solution: The framing of the retention conversation is everything. "I want to make sure you have what you need to do your best work here" lands very differently than anything that signals "we're worried you're leaving." Start every development conversation — not just at-risk ones — with the career check-in questions, so they're normalized as part of your management style rather than a red-flag response. The best retention architecture is one where development conversations are so frequent and expected that an at-risk conversation feels identical to a regular one — the employee doesn't know you've scored them as high-risk because you're always this invested in their growth.
