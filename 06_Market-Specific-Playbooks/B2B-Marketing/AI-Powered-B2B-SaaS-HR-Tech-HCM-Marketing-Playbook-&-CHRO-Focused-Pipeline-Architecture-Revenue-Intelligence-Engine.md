METHODOLOGY: Apply the following HR Tech-specific frameworks in sequence:

**Framework 1 — The HR Champion-to-Economic-Buyer Translation Model (HCEBT)**
HR Tech is one of the few B2B software categories where the product champion is structurally separated from the budget decision. HR practitioners evaluate, love, and recommend your product — but they must then convince a CFO or CEO who views HR software as a cost center, not a strategic asset. Most HR Tech companies lose deals not because HR doesn't love them, but because HR can't make the internal business case.

The HCEBT model has four stages:
Stage 1 — Champion Activation: Make HR practitioners deeply enthusiastic advocates through product experience, community connection, and practitioner-first content
Stage 2 — Internal Justification Arming: Give HR champions everything they need to present the ROI case to CFOs and CEOs — pre-built financial models, board presentation templates, peer benchmarks
Stage 3 — Economic Buyer Direct Engagement: Run parallel marketing programs directly to CFOs and CEOs that frame HR Tech as revenue and efficiency infrastructure, not HR overhead
Stage 4 — Aligned Close: Facilitate the moment when CHRO/VP HR and CFO/CEO are looking at the same ROI narrative simultaneously — this is the moment deals close

Identify which stage your current marketing programs primarily address. Most HR Tech companies are stuck in Stage 1 (practitioner love) without Stage 2 arming or Stage 3 economic buyer engagement. Design programs for all four stages.

**Framework 2 — HR Compliance Trigger Matrix**
HR software purchases are disproportionately driven by compliance urgency and workforce events rather than general technology evaluation. Map the category of triggers most relevant to your product:

WORKFORCE GROWTH TRIGGERS:
- Rapid headcount scaling (50%+ growth in 12 months overwhelms manual HR processes)
- Geographic expansion requiring new state/country compliance
- Shift from founder-led HR to first professional HR team (company crossing 50–100 employees)
- Hybrid/remote workforce mandate requiring new tracking and management systems

COMPLIANCE AND REGULATORY TRIGGERS:
- EEO-1 / OFCCP audit notification or upcoming filing deadline
- Pay equity audit requirement (especially Colorado, California, Illinois, New York law requirements)
- GDPR right-to-erasure audit for employee data
- State-specific FMLA / paid leave law expansion (compliance gap in current system)
- Cyber insurance renewal requiring employee data protection documentation

LEADERSHIP AND ORGANIZATIONAL TRIGGERS:
- New CHRO / VP of HR hire — first 90 days evaluation window
- CEO or Board mandate for workforce analytics dashboard
- M&A requiring workforce integration from two incompatible HR systems
- Performance management overhaul following high-profile talent departure
- Annual benefits renewal cycle (for benefits administration products)

PRODUCT-SPECIFIC TRIGGERS:
- ATS: Hiring velocity increase, candidate experience decline, EEOC compliance gap
- Performance: Annual/semi-annual review cycle approaching, engagement survey results showing manager quality issues
- Payroll: Off-cycle tax compliance error, multi-state expansion, payroll provider contract renewal
- People Analytics: Board asking for workforce metrics the current system can't produce

For each relevant trigger, design a detection methodology (intent signal, job posting signal, news signal, seasonal calendar signal) and a specific outbound campaign sequence.

**Framework 3 — HR Tech Peer Validation Architecture**
HR professionals are among the most community-driven buyers in B2B software. They consult peers before evaluating software, share recommendations in Slack communities, post vendor reviews on G2/Capterra before the sales rep even follows up, and attend conferences specifically to compare notes with peers about their tech stack.

The Peer Validation Architecture has five components:

Component 1 — Review Platform Strategy: G2 and Capterra are where HR practitioners first look. A 4.6+ rating with 200+ reviews from practitioners at companies your ICP recognizes is a prerequisite to being taken seriously. Design a systematic review acquisition program triggered at 30, 90, and 180 days post-implementation, and at annual renewal.

Component 2 — Community Native Presence: Identify the 5–8 communities where your ICP practitioners spend time (e.g., People Ops Professionals, HR Open Source, Lattice community, specific LinkedIn groups, SHRM local chapter groups, Slack communities like Remote HR, Folks HR). Design a community presence strategy that is practitioner-led, not vendor-led.

Component 3 — CHRO Reference Program: Build a formal CHRO reference network — CHROs speaking at conferences, publishing research with you, and available for peer reference calls. For each CHRO reference, define: their company profile, the specific outcome story they can tell, their preferred format (written quote, reference call, conference co-presentation), and the deal stages where they are most effective.

Component 4 — Analyst and Advisory Firm Strategy: Gartner (Magic Quadrant for HCM Suites, Critical Capabilities for Cloud HCM), Forrester (Talent Acquisition Technology landscape), Josh Bersin Company (most influential HR analyst for CHRO audiences), and RedThread Research influence CHRO technology decisions. Map your analyst relations program against the specific evaluation cycles that matter for your category.

Component 5 — HR Practitioner Content Authority: HR practitioners follow a small set of content authorities. Identify the 10 most-followed HR thought leaders on LinkedIn in your category and design a co-creation or sponsorship program to appear in their content distribution. One authentic mention from Adam Grant, Liz Ryan, or Josh Bersin reaches more CHROs than 6 months of LinkedIn advertising.

**Framework 4 — Integration and Data Trust Architecture**
Employee data is the most sensitive data a company holds — it includes compensation, performance ratings, medical leave information, Social Security numbers, and personnel actions that can create legal liability. HR buyers — even technically non-sophisticated HR practitioners — understand this viscerally. Any vendor who cannot immediately demonstrate a credible data security and integration story loses deals at the IT and Legal review stage, even when HR loves the product.

Build the following artifacts:
1. Data Trust One-Pager: A single-page summary of your security certifications (SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, GDPR DPA), data handling practices (encryption at rest/in transit, data residency options, audit logs, right-to-erasure capability), and breach response commitments. This should be ready to send to the IT/CISO reviewer on Day 1 of security review.
2. Integration Ecosystem Map: A visual showing every integration your product natively supports with logos — your HCM, ATS, payroll, SSO, productivity tool (Slack, Teams), and business intelligence tool integrations. HR buyers evaluate software by whether it "plays nice with what we already have." An integration page with 50+ named integrations with logos dramatically reduces IT friction.
3. Implementation Risk Mitigation Program: HR buyers are terrified of bad implementations. HCM migrations are notorious for payroll errors, data loss, and employee experience disasters. Design a "Zero Surprises Implementation" program with: dedicated implementation manager, phased rollout playbook, data migration audit process, parallel run period (running old and new systems simultaneously for the first payroll cycle), and an implementation guarantee with defined success criteria.

**Framework 5 — Suite vs. Best-of-Breed Positioning Architecture**
Every HR Tech deal involves a positioning decision: are you selling a specialized point solution that does one thing exceptionally well, or a platform that consolidates multiple HR functions? Both are valid positioning strategies, but they require completely different messaging architectures.

BEST-OF-BREED POSITIONING (for point solutions competing against suite modules):
Core argument: "The [Category] module inside Workday/SAP/Oracle was built for 1% of HR's needs. We built our entire company around solving [specific problem] for HR teams who deserve world-class [capability] — not an afterthought module."
Proof points: Superior feature depth in your category, NPS scores vs. enterprise HCM suite module alternatives, implementation speed (weeks vs. months for the suite), and customer retention data.
Competitive risk: Buyers worry about integration complexity and vendor sprawl. Counter this with: native integrations with the leading HCM suites (Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle, BambooHR), a data synchronization guarantee, and a "best-of-breed stack vs. all-in-one suite" ROI calculator that makes the integration cost transparent.

PLATFORM CONSOLIDATION POSITIONING (for HCM suite or multi-module platforms):
Core argument: "The average mid-market company runs 6–9 separate HR point solutions with no unified employee record, siloed analytics, and a fragmented employee experience. We consolidate your entire people tech stack into one employee journey."
Proof points: Total cost of ownership reduction (consolidation savings), unified people analytics that cross-functional point solutions can't produce, employee experience improvement (one login, one record, one profile), and implementation simplification.
Competitive risk: Buyers fear that no single platform is best at everything. Counter with: depth scores in G2 and Gartner showing your ratings in each module area, a modular adoption path (start with one module, expand to platform over time), and customer journey stories showing the consolidation ROI.

## Troubleshooting

**Problem: HR champions love the product but deals stall when the CFO review stage begins.**
Solution: This is the defining challenge of HR Tech selling and it means your champion is not equipped to make the internal business case. Diagnose whether the stall is at "CFO won't take the meeting" or "CFO took the meeting but said no." If the CFO won't meet, your champion hasn't framed this as a CFO-level business decision — they've positioned it as an HR tool. Reframe with your champion: this is a workforce productivity investment that will produce [specific ROI]. Give them the one-page CFO summary and role-play the CFO conversation before they present. If the CFO met and said no, it was usually one of three issues: (1) the ROI model was built on assumptions the CFO challenged — solve this by using your benchmark data for every assumption and pre-answering the challenge in the model, (2) the implementation risk narrative was weak — strengthen with your implementation guarantee and reference to customers who completed implementation without disruption, or (3) there's a competing internal priority — in this case, plant a seed for the next budget cycle and sequence the CFO into your executive content program.

**Problem: CHRO response rates are low despite solid messaging and trigger-based outreach.**
Solution: In HR Tech, sender credibility matters more than in almost any other B2B category. CHROs talk to each other constantly at conferences and in peer communities — and they remember good and bad vendor interactions by company. If your outreach is coming from SDRs with 18 months of experience, even a great message will underperform. Restructure your CHRO outreach: Tier 1 CHROs should be outreached by your VP Sales, Head of Marketing, or CEO — not SDRs. Warm introductions from your CHRO advisory board members and reference customers will produce 5–10x the response rate of any cold outreach, regardless of message quality. If you have 5 CHRO references, each one should be actively facilitating 2–3 introductions per quarter to their peers at ICP companies.

**Problem: Research report generated downloads but produced no pipeline.**
Solution: HR practitioner content consumption is high (they love reports and guides) but conversion from download to pipeline is low if the follow-up is generic. Three fixes: (1) Make your follow-up human and specific — within 2 hours of download, a human sends a personal email referencing a specific finding from the report that is relevant to that company's situation. This requires SDRs to read the company profile before outreaching. Generic "thanks for downloading" automation kills conversion. (2) Gate the highest-value section of your report (the benchmarking data or the compliance checklist) behind a short conversation with a practitioner on your team — position it as a "benchmark consultation" not a sales call. (3) Segment your follow-up by company size and industry — a CHRO at a 300-person fintech company has different priorities than a VP HR at a 2,000-person healthcare company; the same follow-up email to both will feel irrelevant to both.
