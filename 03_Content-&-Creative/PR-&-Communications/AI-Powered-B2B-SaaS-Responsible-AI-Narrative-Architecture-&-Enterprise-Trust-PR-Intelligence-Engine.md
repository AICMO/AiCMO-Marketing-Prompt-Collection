**DELIVERABLE 3: CISO AI SAFETY BRIEFING — THE 8-QUESTION TEMPLATE**

Build a concise, structured AI Safety Summary formatted as: Question → Direct Answer → Evidence/Reference

Format requirements:
- 1 page maximum (front side only — executives read one pages, not packets)
- Plain language: no jargon, no marketing copy
- Every answer cites a specific document, policy, or control — not a general reassurance
- Designed to be used as a pre-meeting leave-behind and a post-meeting follow-up confirmation

THE 8 QUESTIONS AND ANSWER FRAMEWORK:

Q1: What data does [COMPANY NAME]'s AI process, and where is it stored?
Answer structure: [Specific data types listed by name] / [Data residency: US/EU/customer-specified — not "flexible"] / [Certifications: SOC 2 Type II Control reference, ISO 27001 if applicable]

Q2: Does [COMPANY NAME] train its AI models on customer data?
Answer structure: [Yes/No with specific policy] / [If yes: opt-in or opt-out, anonymization method, retention period, contractual guarantee] / [If no: what data IS used for training and from what source]
Note: CISOs are more concerned about unclear policies than about "yes if" — a clear "yes, with these specific controls" beats a vague "no" they can't verify

Q3: How does [COMPANY NAME] protect against AI-specific attacks (prompt injection, model extraction, adversarial inputs)?
Answer structure: [Specific technical controls, not categories] / [Testing methodology: red team, third-party pen test] / [Third-party security audit status and most recent date]

Q4: What happens to our data if we terminate the contract?
Answer structure: [Specific deletion timeline: "within 30 days of termination"] / [Format of data export provided to customer] / [Certification of deletion: yes/no, and how it's delivered] / [Reference: Contract Section X.X]

Q5: How is [COMPANY NAME]'s AI monitored in production for bias, drift, or unexpected behavior?
Answer structure: [Monitoring methodology: what metrics, what thresholds] / [Monitoring owner: internal team name or third-party] / [What triggers a model review, rollback, or customer notification]

Q6: Has [COMPANY NAME]'s AI ever produced harmful or incorrect outputs, and how was that handled?
Answer structure: [Honest answer with specifics — an honest "yes with resolution" is more credible than "none reported" if incidents exist] / [Incident response process: who was notified, how fast, what documentation was provided] / [Link to incident log in Transparency Report if published]
Note: This question separates mature AI vendors from immature ones. Having had an incident and handled it well is more trustworthy than claiming a perfect record

Q7: Is [COMPANY NAME] subject to the EU AI Act or other AI-specific regulations, and are you compliant?
Answer structure: [Specific regulation applicability: is your AI high-risk under EU AI Act Article 6?] / [Current compliance status: compliant, in progress, or planned with timeline] / [Customer-specific applicability: what this means for their specific deployment scenario]

Q8: Who at [COMPANY NAME] is accountable for AI safety, and can our security team speak with them?
Answer structure: [Named role: CAIO, CTO, Head of AI Safety — specific title, not "our security team"] / [Contact process for security-specific questions: direct email, not info@] / [Reference customer whose CISO will take a call and vouch for the engagement process — the most powerful trust signal available]

**DELIVERABLE 5: AI INCIDENT CRISIS COMMUNICATIONS FRAMEWORK**

Build a tiered response protocol — because how you handle AI incidents in private determines your credibility when they become public:

TIER 1 INCIDENT — INTERNAL (No customer impact, contained before output reaches customer):
Definition: AI model produces unexpected, incorrect, or anomalous outputs caught in internal QA or monitoring before any customer impact
Response protocol:
- Trigger: Any team member can escalate by emailing AI-INCIDENTS@[company] — 4-hour acknowledgment SLA from AI Safety team
- Investigation: Root cause analysis completed within 5 business days; assigned owner is Head of AI Safety or CTO directly
- Documentation: Incident logged in internal AI Incident Register (date, model, anomaly description, root cause, resolution, prevention measure)
- External communications: None required unless customer contracts require proactive disclosure of model behavior changes; legal counsel reviews within 24 hours to confirm
- Model card update: If incident reveals documented limitation not previously described, model card updated within 2 weeks

TIER 2 INCIDENT — CUSTOMER-FACING (Incorrect outputs reached one or more customers, contained within 48 hours):
Definition: AI model produced incorrect outputs that were delivered to customers before being identified; impact contained, no public knowledge
Response protocol:
- Customer notification: Personal outreach within 24 hours from VP Customer Success or equivalent — not automated, not template-only; specific description of what happened, what data was affected, what was done to resolve it
- Root cause communication: Written RCA delivered to affected customer(s) within 5 business days; offer a call to walk through it
- Regulatory review: Legal counsel review within 4 hours to determine if GDPR, CCPA, or sector-specific breach notification is required
- PR posture: Proactive disclosure to journalists if incident is likely to become independently discoverable; do not wait for journalist inquiry — proactive disclosure is always better than reactive denial
- Holding statement (adapt for use): "[COMPANY NAME] identified an issue with [specific model behavior] that affected [number] customers between [date range]. We notified affected customers directly within 24 hours, implemented [specific technical fix] on [date], and are providing full transparency through our AI incident disclosure policy. [Named contact] is available to speak with customers or media with questions."
- Remediation offer: Review of all AI outputs from the affected period + offer an independent accuracy audit for affected customers

TIER 3 INCIDENT — PUBLIC (Incident has become public knowledge, journalist has inquired, or regulatory body has contacted):
Definition: AI incident is either already published by media, has been reported to a regulatory agency, or is the subject of a journalist inquiry that will likely result in coverage
First 4-hour response protocol:
Hour 1: CEO notification + outside legal counsel engagement + PR firm brief (if agency relationship exists) OR senior in-house comms takes lead
Hour 2: Incident command assembled: CEO, General Counsel, CTO, Head of Communications, VP Customer Success — everyone in one call, decision authority clear from the start
Hour 3: Proactive customer notification to ALL enterprise customers (not just affected) — the goal is that every customer hears this from [COMPANY NAME] before they read about it in the press; brief, factual, honest
Hour 4: First public statement published on company newsroom and sent proactively to any journalists who have contacted the company — do not wait for requests

Core principles for Tier 3 communications:
- Lead with what happened, what you did, and what you're doing — not with defending the AI or minimizing the impact
- Name an internal accountability owner in all public statements (a real person, not "the company")
- Do not make claims about root cause until RCA is complete; use "we are investigating" — not "we believe this was caused by..."
- Commit publicly to a post-incident review within 30 days; publish that review regardless of what it says
- Brief key analyst contacts (Gartner, Forrester) within 24 hours — they will be asked for comment and you want them informed by you first

## Troubleshooting

**Problem: Marketing and Legal can't agree on what to publish in the AI Transparency Report — Legal wants to say nothing; Marketing wants full transparency**
Solution: Bypass the Transparency Report debate entirely and start with the AI Safety Summary 1-pager. The 1-pager answers specific questions with specific factual statements — far lower legal risk than a broad transparency narrative because each statement is precise and pre-validated. Get the 1-pager approved, deploy it in three enterprise security reviews, and measure the cycle reduction. When you have quantified evidence that "AI Safety Summary sent = 30 fewer days to close," you have the business case to unlock Legal's approval for the full report. In the meantime, publish a commitment on your Responsible AI page: "AI Transparency Report coming [specific quarter]" — the public commitment creates internal urgency that legal review timelines alone do not.

**Problem: We had a real AI incident that wasn't handled well — disclosure was delayed and the customer was frustrated. We're worried that being transparent about it now in our Transparency Report will hurt us**
Solution: Proactive disclosure of past incidents, handled with professional specificity, almost always builds rather than destroys enterprise trust. The formula: (1) Name the incident precisely — date, model, what happened; (2) Describe exactly what you did to fix it, including what didn't work initially; (3) Describe the specific monitoring or process change that prevents recurrence. Enterprise buyers who would disqualify you for having had an incident will disqualify you anyway when they discover it later — and they will discover it. Enterprise buyers who respond to professional incident handling (the majority) will trust you more for publishing it than they would if you claimed a perfect record. The risk of a journalist or prospective customer discovering an undisclosed incident during a competitive deal is far higher than the risk of publishing a well-handled incident history in your own Transparency Report.

**Problem: Executives are reluctant to publish thought leadership that could be used against them by regulators or plaintiffs' attorneys**
Solution: Submit the 3 thought leadership angles to Legal for review and specifically ask them to red-line any claims that create liability exposure — do not ask them whether to do it, ask them how to do it safely. The angles most likely to create legal risk are those making specific compliance claims (angle 3, regulatory) or naming competitors (avoid naming in any executive byline — let readers connect the dots). Angles 1 and 2 can be written entirely as practitioner opinion ("here's what we believe enterprise buyers should demand") rather than factual compliance assertions, which eliminates most legal risk while retaining the credibility impact. A competent comms and legal partnership can produce enterprise AI thought leadership that builds market authority and is defensible under scrutiny — the goal is not to avoid the conversation but to own it responsibly.
